We thank the reviewer for the positive assessment and the precise questions.

**W1 — L174 "models exhaust useful thinking"; only Low/Mid for Budget Forcing.** Budget Forcing was run only at Low and Mid because at higher budgets it degenerates into repetitive self-termination (App. C): once the end-of-thinking token is suppressed and wait-strings are injected past the point where the model naturally stops, the additional tokens are repetition rather than new reasoning, and realized quality is already flat-to-negative at Low/Mid (Table 3). We operationalize "useful thinking exhausted" as: additional forced thinking tokens do not raise realized quality and instead produce degenerate output. We will reword L174 to state this and cross-reference App. C in the camera-ready.

We will add a pointer from the Table 3 caption to Table 8.

**W3 — how is cosine similarity computed / which embedding model?** The diversity metric in Sec. 6.2 is the mean pairwise cosine distance between final outputs, computed with [[FILL BEFORE POSTING: exact embedding model + version, whether over full final outputs, and pooling, e.g. "sentence embeddings from all-MiniLM-L6-v2, mean-pooled over tokens"]]. We will state this in App. C.

**Minor.** We will define ORM (Outcome Reward Model) at first use (currently first appears at L78), and enlarge the text in Figures 1 and 5.

**Q1 — is judge-noise inflation a property of the judge, the task, or both, and how does that affect the correction?** Both, and the correction handles either. We estimate the judge variance sigma_J empirically by scoring the *same* generation four times per entry (App. F), so sigma_J captures the total variance of the judge's realized score at fixed sampling temperature — arising from both genuine judge stochasticity and interpretive latitude in the rubric. The estimator corrects for this measured variance regardless of its source: a more interpretation-heavy rubric simply yields a larger sigma_J (cf. WritingBench vs PRBench, Table 14) and a correspondingly larger correction. Importantly, App. F.2 shows the identity h ~= rho_v is invariant to sigma_J — noise attenuates numerator and denominator by the same factor and cancels — so the source of inflation changes the magnitude of the oracle correction but not the central selection-failure result.

**Q2 — is the Fusion/SR difference mostly the prompt + input pool; would an SR-style prompt inside Fusion close the headroom gap?** The difference is structural, not merely the prompt. Fusion is a single synthesis call conditioned on N independent, parallel candidates; SR is sequential critique-then-rewrite over the correlated drafts of a single trajectory (K=3 history window). The ~40% (Fusion) vs ~15% (BoN) capture gap is between synthesis and external-verifier selection, not between two prompts, so inserting an SR-style critique prompt into a Fusion call would not be expected to move it. Sec. 6.4 supports this: Fusion's advantage relative to a random candidate is structural (it bypasses selection) and does not grow with model capability. We would be glad to run this as an ablation on 1–2 benchmarks if the reviewer finds it useful, but we expect it to track direct-synthesis Fusion.

**Q3 — RefineBench respects [TERMINATE]; you force iterations and ignore STOP.** We force iterations so SR meets the same fixed token budgets as every other method — respecting stop signals gives variable per-item compute and breaks the compute-normalized comparison that is central to the paper. We ran the respect-STOP variant: re-selecting each SR output at the iteration where the stop-check first returned STOP leaves realized scores essentially unchanged. So the HealthBench/LEXam regressions are not an artifact of ignoring stop signals — refinement genuinely fails to improve (and often degrades) on these tasks whether or not STOP is respected, and respecting [TERMINATE] as in Lee et al. does not recover performance in our setting. We therefore handle the verbosity confound analytically rather than by intervention: App. O quantifies it under both our unified judge and the WritingBench native critic and shows the WritingBench "gain" is attributable to length, not quality. We agree a length-controlled refinement protocol is a reasonable direction and will note it as future work; keeping prompting uniform across methods is what makes the compute-matched comparison fair.

We hope these clarifications, together with the third-family and judge results we will post here, address the reviewer's concerns, and we are happy to answer follow-ups.

We thank the reviewer. The central concern — that we claim Fusion is fundamentally superior to selection — rests on a framing we would like to correct: our primary claim is the *exploitation bottleneck*, not Fusion's superiority. Fusion is the only method above baseline on every benchmark for one model family; it still recovers only ~40% of available headroom, and it regresses on OLMo3-32B (Tables 22/28). It is an existence proof that exploitation can partially work by bypassing the external verifier — not a recommended system.

**Fairness of the BoN-vs-Fusion comparison.** BoN and Fusion receive the same total token budget (Sec. 4, Table 1) and draw from the same candidate pool: Fusion synthesizes over a random subset of the precomputed BoN-16 pool (App. C). So the comparison is not about compute or candidate count. Fig. 5(left) isolates the remaining "is this just variance?" question by comparing the fused output against a held-out i.i.d. candidate drawn from *outside* the shared sub-pool, at equal compute; the fused output wins on all five benchmarks (Wilcoxon p<0.001). Synthesis raises mean candidate quality, not just the upper tail.

**"This only proves a strong generator fuses better than a weak verifier selects."** By Eq. 4 (App. E), a selection method's headroom capture equals its verifier correlation, h ~= rho_v; on open-ended tasks rho_v ~= 0.12 (Table 30), so selection is near-random in this regime — by the structure of the task, not by our RM choice. Crucially, this failure is regime-specific, not universal: the same selection methods scale cleanly on verifiable tasks (MATH-500/GPQA, App. L) and when the generator is weak relative to the RM (App. M, reproducing the Skywork-RM paper's own scaling with that paper's weaker generator). So the comparison is not rigged — the verifier works exactly where its training distribution applies and fails where it does not, and that regime-dependence is the finding. Generative exploitation that bypasses the verifier does better but still leaves most headroom unrealized (Fusion ~40%).

**"Handicapped BoN/tree search with outdated dense Llama RMs vs. frontier MoE verifiers."** Both ORMs were selected as top RewardBench 2 performers (Sec. 4), not to handicap BoN. Two RMs from different base families fail near-identically (overall XHigh both 0.584; mean rho_v 0.120 Skywork, 0.107 Llama, Table 30) — the signature of a structural limit rather than a single weak model. To directly test the stronger verifier the reviewer has in mind, we are running the generator itself as a **single-token reward model** (prompting it for a 1–10 score and selecting the argmax). We keep it single-token by design: our compute normalization treats verifier cost as negligible (Sec. 4), and a reward model that *reasons* before scoring would consume generator tokens and would no longer be compute-matched to a discriminative ORM. To avoid circularity with our Qwen judge, we report this verifier's correlation against the HealthBench physician annotations (human ground truth, Table 12), with judge-based correlations elsewhere as a consistency check. We will post the results in this thread. We note that Eq. 4 makes the conclusion RM-agnostic: a stronger verifier shifts rho_v, but the achievable headroom capture still equals that correlation.

**"Why only rely on RewardBench 2 models, not general frontier models?"** Because RewardBench 2 is the field-standard, non-cherry-picked selection criterion for outcome reward models, which gives a defensible and reproducible choice; and because Eq. 4 makes the ceiling RM-agnostic (capture = correlation). The generative single-token reward-model experiment above extends beyond RewardBench 2 models to the stronger in-family verifier the reviewer suggests, and we will report what correlation and headroom capture it achieves.

We hope this clarifies that the paper's claim is the exploitation bottleneck (supported across all five families), that the BoN/Fusion comparison is compute- and pool-matched, and that the selection failure is a measured, regime-specific property predicted by rho_v — not an artifact of RM choice. We are happy to discuss further.
