# Wildlife Tracking & Field Ecology — Domain Specification

Reference document for building a tagged animal state simulator. All numbers are sourced from peer-reviewed literature, USFWS/NPS publications, and established wildlife management protocols.

## 1. Species Profiles

### 1.1 Gray Wolf (Canis lupus)

**Physical characteristics:**
- Male weight: 70–145 lbs (32–65 kg); Yellowstone average 110 lbs (50 kg)
- Female weight: 60–100 lbs (27–45 kg); Yellowstone average 90 lbs (41 kg)
- Body length: 4.5–6.5 ft (1.4–2.0 m) nose to tail tip
- Shoulder height: 26–32 inches (66–81 cm)
- Lifespan: 6–8 years in the wild; up to 13 years (rare)

**Pack structure:**
- Typical pack size: 5–12 individuals (average 8 in Yellowstone)
- Led by a breeding pair (formerly called "alpha pair")
- Pack composition: breeding pair, yearlings, pups, occasionally unrelated immigrants
- Pack turnover: 15–30% annually from dispersal, death, and immigration

**Territory:**
- Home range: 100–500 sq mi (260–1,300 km²) in the Northern Rockies
- Smaller ranges (25–100 sq mi) in prey-dense areas (Great Lakes)
- Larger ranges (up to 1,000+ sq mi) in tundra/taiga with migratory caribou
- Territory overlap between adjacent packs: typically < 5% (buffer zones exist)
- Boundary marking: scent posts every 300–500 m along territory edges

**Prey species (Yellowstone seasonal breakdown):**
- Winter: elk (>96%), bison (3–4%), deer (1.5%)
- Spring: elk (89%), bison (7%), deer (7.1%)
- Summer: elk (85%), bison (14.1%), deer (<1%)
- Supplementary prey: beavers, rabbits, rodents, ground squirrels

**Movement patterns:**
- Average daily movement: 15–30 km (9–19 mi) during normal activity
- Males average: 27.6 km/day; females average: 22.1 km/day
- Maximum single-day movement: 72 km (45 mi) or more in pursuit of prey
- Pre-dispersal daily displacement: rarely exceeds 5 km
- During dispersal: mean daily displacement 16.8 km
- Typical dispersal distance: 80–160 km (50–100 mi) from natal pack
- Maximum documented dispersal: 886 km (550 mi)
- Dispersal age: 1–2 years (at sexual maturity)

**Breeding and denning:**
- Breeding season: late January–early March (peak February)
- Gestation: 63 days
- Litter size: 4–6 pups (range: 1–11)
- Pup birth weight: ~1 lb (0.45 kg)
- Pups born blind and deaf; eyes open at 10–15 days
- Pup weight at 2 weeks: ~4 lbs (1.8 kg)
- Den types: excavated under roots, boulders, hillsides; caves with tunnel to enlarged chamber
- Den emergence: pups begin leaving den at 3–4 weeks
- Rendezvous sites used from ~8 weeks until pups travel with pack (~3–4 months old)

### 1.2 Grizzly Bear (Ursus arctos horribilis)

**Physical characteristics:**
- Male weight (spring, post-hibernation): 300–450 lbs (136–204 kg)
- Male weight (fall, pre-hibernation): 400–790 lbs (180–360 kg)
- Female weight (spring): 200–280 lbs (90–127 kg)
- Female weight (fall): 250–450 lbs (113–204 kg)
- Coastal males average: ~899 lbs (408 kg) at peak
- Inland males average: ~600 lbs (272 kg) at peak
- Body length: 6–7 ft (1.8–2.1 m)
- Shoulder height: 3–3.5 ft (0.9–1.1 m) on all fours; 6–7 ft standing
- Lifespan: 20–25 years in the wild

**Social structure:**
- Primarily solitary except: mothers with cubs, mating pairs (briefly), aggregations at food sources (salmon streams, garbage)
- Dominance hierarchy at shared feeding sites: large males > smaller males > females with cubs > subadults
- Cubs stay with mother for 2–3 years
- Females with cubs-of-the-year are highly aggressive and avoid adult males

**Territory / Home range:**
- Male home range: 200–600 sq mi (500–1,500 km²)
- Female home range: 50–150 sq mi (130–390 km²)
- Coastal bears with salmon access: smaller ranges (food density)
- Home ranges overlap extensively (not truly territorial like wolves)

**Hibernation:**
- Den entry: October–December (females with cubs enter earliest)
- Den emergence: males in early-to-mid March; females with cubs in April–May
- Hibernation duration: 4–6 months
- Weight loss during hibernation: ~30% of body weight
- Heart rate drops from 40 bpm to 8–10 bpm
- Body temperature drops only slightly: 88–93°F (vs. normal 100°F)

**Feeding and seasonal behavior:**
- Hyperphagia: late July–October, consuming 20,000+ calories/day
- Weight gain during hyperphagia: up to 3.6 lbs/day (1.65 kg/day)
- Total pre-hibernation weight gain: up to 400 lbs (180 kg)
- Salmon runs: primary food source July–December (peak August–October)
- Spring diet: roots, grasses, carrion (winter-killed ungulates), ground squirrels
- Summer diet: berries, insects, whitebark pine nuts, ungulate calves

**Movement patterns:**
- Daily movement: 2–10 km typical; up to 40 km when seeking food
- Seasonal movement: some bears travel 100+ km between spring and fall ranges
- Young males disperse 50–100 km from natal area

### 1.3 Rocky Mountain Elk (Cervus canadensis)

**Physical characteristics:**
- Bull weight: 600–1,000 lbs (270–450 kg); average ~700 lbs (318 kg)
- Cow weight: 400–600 lbs (180–270 kg); average ~500 lbs (227 kg)
- Bulls lose up to 20% of body weight during rut
- Body length: 6.5–8.5 ft (2.0–2.6 m)
- Shoulder height: 4.5–5.0 ft (1.4–1.5 m) for bulls
- Lifespan: 10–15 years in the wild

**Herd structure:**
- Winter herds: 50–several hundred animals (mixed sex)
- Summer: cows and calves in groups of 10–25; bulls in bachelor groups of 2–8
- Rut: bulls gather harems of 10–30 cows
- Spring dispersal into smaller groups at intermediate elevations

**Antler cycle:**
- Antler drop: late February–April
- New growth begins: April–May (covered in velvet)
- Velvet shed: August–September (rubbing against trees)
- Mature bull antler weight: up to 40 lbs (18 kg) per set
- Antler growth rate: up to 1 inch per day during peak growth

**Calving:**
- Breeding season (rut): mid-September through mid-October
- Gestation: ~250 days (~8.3 months)
- Calving: late May–mid June (synchronized)
- Single calf is typical; twins are rare
- Calf birth weight: 30–40 lbs (14–18 kg)
- Calves spotted at birth; spots fade by 3–4 months

**Migration:**
- Vertical migration: low-elevation winter range to high-elevation summer range
- Migration distance: 10–100+ miles (16–160+ km) one way
- Spring migration triggered by snowmelt and green-up
- Fall migration triggered by deep/crusted snow
- Some populations are resident (non-migratory)
- Migration duration: < 1 day (short, high-elevation routes) to several months (long-distance)

**Movement patterns:**
- Daily movement: 2–10 km typical
- Winter: reduced movement, energy conservation
- Summer: more dispersed, 5–15 km/day
- Home range: 5–30 sq mi (13–78 km²) for cows; 10–50+ sq mi for bulls

### 1.4 Mountain Lion / Cougar (Puma concolor)

**Physical characteristics:**
- Male weight: 115–220 lbs (52–100 kg); average ~140 lbs (64 kg)
- Female weight: 64–141 lbs (29–64 kg); average ~100 lbs (45 kg)
- Body length (nose to tail): 5–9 ft (1.5–2.7 m); tail accounts for ~3 ft
- Shoulder height: 24–35 inches (60–90 cm)
- Lifespan: 8–13 years in the wild

**Social structure:**
- Strictly solitary except mothers with kittens and brief mating encounters
- Males are intolerant of other males in their territory
- Females tolerate overlap with other females and with the resident male

**Territory / Home range:**
- Male territory: 50–150 sq mi (130–390 km²)
- Female territory: 25–75 sq mi (65–195 km²) — roughly half of males
- Territory maintained by scrapes (dirt mounds), urine, and feces
- Overlap between female home ranges is common

**Hunting and prey:**
- Kill frequency: approximately 1 deer-sized animal per 7–10 days
- Stays near kill for 3–8 days, feeding intermittently and caching with debris
- Primary prey: mule deer, white-tailed deer, elk calves
- Supplementary prey: raccoons, rabbits, porcupines, bighorn sheep, feral hogs

**Reproduction:**
- Breeding: year-round, but peaks in winter/spring
- Gestation: 82–96 days (average ~90 days)
- Litter size: 1–6 kittens (average 2–3)
- Kitten birth weight: ~1 lb (0.45 kg)
- Kittens spotted at birth; spots fade by 12–18 months
- Kittens stay with mother: 12–24 months
- Dispersal: males 100–274 km; females up to 140 km

**Movement patterns:**
- Daily movement: 3–15 km; males move farther than females
- Crepuscular and nocturnal — most movement at dawn/dusk
- Can travel 40+ km in a single night during dispersal

### 1.5 Bald Eagle (Haliaeetus leucocephalus)

**Physical characteristics:**
- Weight: 6.5–14 lbs (3–6.3 kg); females ~25% larger than males
- Male weight: 6.5–9 lbs (3–4 kg)
- Female weight: 10–14 lbs (4.5–6.3 kg)
- Wingspan: 5.9–7.5 ft (1.8–2.3 m)
- Body length: 28–40 inches (71–102 cm)
- Lifespan: 20–30 years in the wild

**Nesting:**
- Nest size: 4–5 ft diameter, 2–4 ft deep; can grow to 10 ft wide and weigh 2,000 lbs after years of additions
- Nest location: large trees (often cottonwoods, ponderosa pines) or cliff faces near water
- Nesting territory: > 1 sq mi; includes active nest + alternate nests
- Pairs are monogamous and may use the same nest for decades
- Average clutch size: 1.87 eggs (range: 1–3)
- Incubation: 34–36 days; both parents incubate
- Fledging: 10–14 weeks after hatching
- Post-fledging dependency: 4–11 weeks (young stay near nest, fed by parents)

**Seasonal movement:**
- Some breeders are year-round residents near nesting territory (if food available)
- Non-breeders and subadults may migrate 100s–1,000s of miles
- Northward migration: April–August
- Southward migration: late July–December
- Congregate along rivers/lakes for salmon runs and open water in winter
- Adult plumage (white head/tail) achieved at 4–5 years

**Movement patterns:**
- Daily foraging range: 5–30 km from nest during breeding
- Soaring/hunting altitude: 50–3,000 m
- Flight speed: 30–35 mph cruising; up to 100 mph diving

### 1.6 Loggerhead Sea Turtle (Caretta caretta)

**Physical characteristics:**
- Adult weight: 170–350 lbs (77–159 kg); average ~250 lbs (113 kg)
- Carapace length: 33–43 inches (84–110 cm)
- Lifespan: 50–67 years (estimated)
- Sexual maturity: 20–35 years

**Nesting:**
- Nesting season: May–August (southeastern US)
- Clutch size: 100–130 eggs (average 116 for loggerheads)
- Egg size: ping-pong-ball-sized (~40 mm diameter)
- Nests per season: 3–6 clutches, spaced ~14 days apart
- Incubation: 55–60 days
- Hatchling survival to adulthood: ~1 in 1,000
- Nest temperature determines sex: <81.5°F = male; >88°F = female; between = mixed
- Nesting interval (years between reproductive seasons): 2–3 years

**Migration:**
- Trans-oceanic migrations: 1,000–12,000+ km
- Japan to Baja California: ~12,000 km round trip
- Post-nesting: return to foraging grounds (often 100s–1,000s of km from nest beach)
- Hatchlings enter oceanic phase ("lost years"): drift in major current systems (e.g., Gulf Stream, Kuroshio) for 7–15 years
- Navigation: geomagnetic map sense, chemical cues, ocean currents

**Oceanic phase:**
- Hatchlings shelter in floating Sargassum mats
- Feed on surface pelagic invertebrates and vegetation
- Largely inaccessible to researchers during this phase
- Transition to neritic (coastal) phase at carapace length ~40–60 cm

**Movement patterns:**
- Oceanic juvenile: passive drift + active swimming; 10–50 km/day with currents
- Adult foraging: 5–20 km/day in coastal waters
- Migratory transit: 20–50 km/day
- Inter-nesting movement: 10–80 km from nesting beach

## 2. GPS Collar / Tag Technology

### 2.1 Fix Interval (How Often Location is Recorded)

| Setting | Use Case | Battery Impact |
|---------|----------|---------------|
| Every 15–20 min | Intensive behavioral studies (predation events, fine-scale movement) | Very high (weeks–months of battery) |
| Every 30 min | Path length / home range estimation (optimal trade-off) | High (months) |
| Every 1 hour | Standard movement studies | Moderate (6–18 months) |
| Every 4 hours | Long-term monitoring, broad-scale movement | Low (2–4 years) |
| Every 8 hours | Minimum for population monitoring | Very low (3–5+ years) |

Note: If the time lag between fix attempts is short, the GPS unit can reuse satellite ephemeris data, reducing time/power per fix and increasing fix success rate.

### 2.2 Battery Life

| Collar Type | Typical Battery Life | Notes |
|-------------|---------------------|-------|
| VHF only | 2–5 years | Beacon transmits continuously; lightweight |
| GPS/Store-on-board | 1–3 years | Data downloaded manually on recapture or drop-off |
| GPS/GSM (cellular) | 6–18 months | High battery drain from cell transmissions |
| GPS/Satellite (Iridium/Argos) | 1–3 years | Moderate drain; works in remote areas |
| GPS/VHF combo | 1–4 years | VHF for field tracking; GPS for data |
| Solar-assisted GPS | 3–7 years | Solar panel recharges battery; best for large animals in open habitat |

### 2.3 GPS Accuracy

| Condition | Accuracy (CEP 95%) |
|-----------|-------------------|
| Open terrain, clear sky | 3–5 meters |
| Open forest / grassland | 5–15 meters |
| Dense canopy (conifer forest) | 15–30 meters |
| Deep canyon / cliff faces | 30–100+ meters (or fix failure) |
| Urban / built environment | 5–20 meters |
| Under water (marine tags) | No GPS fix; Argos/acoustic only |

Fix success rate drops significantly in dense canopy and canyon terrain. Studies report 60–85% fix success in forested environments vs. 95%+ in open terrain.

### 2.4 Collar/Tag Types by Species

| Technology | Species | Weight | Data |
|-----------|---------|--------|------|
| VHF radio collar | Wolves, bears, elk, cougars | 200–800 g | Direction/proximity only; manual tracking |
| GPS/GSM collar | Wolves, elk, bears (where cell coverage exists) | 300–1,200 g | High-res location data via cell network |
| GPS/Iridium satellite collar | Wolves, bears, elk, cougars (remote areas) | 400–1,500 g | Location data via Iridium satellite |
| GPS/Argos satellite collar | Bears, large ungulates | 500–1,500 g | Lower resolution than Iridium; global coverage |
| Platform Transmitter Terminal (PTT) | Bald eagles, raptors | 30–100 g (backpack or tail-mount) | Argos satellite; lower accuracy (~250 m–1.5 km) |
| GPS/GSM backpack transmitter | Eagles, large birds | 40–80 g | Solar powered; cellular or satellite upload |
| Satellite tag (SPOT/PAT) | Sea turtles | 50–150 g (epoxy-mounted on carapace) | Argos locations when surfacing; pop-off archival tags store depth/temp |
| Acoustic transmitter | Sea turtles, fish | 5–50 g (internal or external) | Detected by hydrophone arrays; no real-time tracking |

### 2.5 Data Transmission Methods

- **Manual download:** VHF-located animal recaptured or collar dropped via timed release; data downloaded via cable. Cheapest but labor-intensive.
- **UHF/VHF proximity download:** Researcher drives within 500 m–2 km; data beamed via radio to handheld receiver. No recapture needed.
- **Cellular (GSM/GPRS/LTE):** Collar transmits via cell network to web portal. Requires cell coverage. High data volume possible.
- **Iridium satellite:** Global coverage. Collar uploads to Iridium constellation; data available on web portal within hours. Most reliable for remote areas.
- **Argos satellite:** Global coverage but lower accuracy and data throughput than Iridium. Common for marine species.
- **LoRa/Sigfox:** Emerging low-power wide-area networks. Small form factor, extended battery, but limited infrastructure.

## 3. Body Condition Scoring (BCS) Systems

### 3.1 Ungulate BCS (1–5 Scale — Adapted from Riney/Cook)

Used for elk, deer, moose. Based on rump fat thickness and visual/palpable assessment.

| Score | Description | Rump Fat (mm) | Approximate Body Fat % |
|-------|-------------|---------------|----------------------|
| 1.0 | **Emaciated.** Hip bones, spine, ribs sharply visible. No subcutaneous fat. Muscle wasting obvious. Animal at risk of death. | 0 mm | <5% |
| 2.0 | **Thin.** Hip bones and spine visible but not sharp. Ribs palpable with slight pressure. Minimal fat cover. | 1–5 mm | 5–10% |
| 3.0 | **Moderate.** Hip bones palpable but not visible. Ribs require firm pressure to feel. Smooth body contour. | 6–15 mm | 10–15% |
| 4.0 | **Good.** Rounded body contour. Hip bones difficult to palpate. Fat deposits visible along rump and brisket. | 16–30 mm | 15–20% |
| 5.0 | **Excellent/Fat.** Very rounded. Hip bones buried under fat. Fat deposits prominent on rump, brisket, base of tail. Animal in peak pre-winter condition. | >30 mm | >20% |

Elk weight correlation (approximate, adult cow):
- BCS 1.0: ~370 lbs (168 kg)
- BCS 3.0: ~475 lbs (215 kg) — normal late summer
- BCS 5.0: ~550 lbs (250 kg) — peak fall condition

### 3.2 Canid/Ursid BCS (1–9 Scale)

Used for wolves, bears, and large canids. More granular than the 5-point scale.

| Score | Category | Description |
|-------|----------|-------------|
| 1 | Emaciated | Ribs, spine, hip bones extremely prominent. Severe muscle wasting. No palpable fat. |
| 2 | Very thin | Ribs easily visible. Prominent spine and hip bones. Minimal muscle mass. |
| 3 | Thin | Ribs easily palpable with minimal fat cover. Waist obvious from above. Abdominal tuck pronounced. |
| 4 | Underweight | Ribs palpable without excess fat cover. Waist easily noted from above. |
| 5 | Ideal | Ribs palpable beneath thin fat layer. Waist visible from above. Abdominal tuck present. |
| 6 | Overweight | Ribs palpable with slight excess fat. Waist discernible but not prominent. |
| 7 | Heavy | Ribs difficult to palpate under moderate fat cover. Waist barely visible. Fat deposits over lumbar area and tail base. |
| 8 | Obese | Ribs not palpable without significant pressure. No waist visible. Prominent fat deposits. |
| 9 | Morbidly obese | Massive fat deposits over thorax, spine, and limbs. Abdomen distended. Difficulty moving. |

Wolf weight correlation (approximate, adult male):
- BCS 3: ~75 lbs (34 kg) — post-winter, thin
- BCS 5: ~110 lbs (50 kg) — healthy
- BCS 7: ~130 lbs (59 kg) — peak fall condition (rare in wild)

Grizzly bear BCS note: Bears naturally cycle between BCS 3–4 in spring (post-hibernation) and BCS 7–8 in fall (pre-hibernation). A spring bear at BCS 5 would be unusually fat; a fall bear at BCS 4 would be dangerously thin.

### 3.3 Bear-Specific Visual BCS (Cattet 2002, adapted)

Used from camera traps or field observation. 5-point scale:

| Score | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| 1 | Skeletal outline visible; shoulder blades, spine, hip bones protruding. Skin draped loosely. |
| 2 | Lean; body contour angular. Some muscle definition visible. Limbs appear thin relative to body. |
| 3 | Moderate; smooth body contour. No bones visible. Limbs proportional. |
| 4 | Robust; rounded body contour. Fat visible as bulging along flanks and rump. Belly sag beginning. |
| 5 | Very fat; heavily rounded. Fat rolls visible. Belly nearly touching ground when walking. Waddling gait. |

## 4. Gray Wolf Seasonal Behavior Patterns (Monthly)

### January
- Late pre-breeding period; increased howling and scent-marking along territory boundaries
- Pack cohesion high; traveling and hunting as a unit
- Snow conditions favor wolves (crusted snow supports wolves but not elk)
- Movement: 20–30 km/day; focused on winter kill sites
- Prey: elk (>96%), weakened by deep snow

### February
- **Breeding season peak.** Breeding pair mates; increased aggression toward subordinates who attempt to breed
- Intense territorial marking: raised-leg urination every 300–500 m on boundaries
- Pack fights and territory incursions most common now
- Some subordinate females come into estrus but are suppressed by dominant female
- Movement: 15–25 km/day; slightly reduced during mating

### March
- Post-breeding; pregnant female becomes less active
- Female begins selecting and excavating den site
- Pack continues winter hunting; carcass sites visited repeatedly
- Early elk calves born in some southern populations
- Movement: 15–25 km/day

### April
- **Denning.** Alpha female enters den ~63 days after breeding
- Pups born (typically mid-April in Northern Rockies)
- Litter size: 4–6 pups born blind, deaf, and helpless
- Pack movement restricted to den area; 1–2 wolves stay near den while others hunt
- Other pack members bring food back to den (regurgitation for pups starting at ~3 weeks)
- Movement of pack: reduced to 5–15 km/day radius from den

### May
- **Pup rearing.** Pups' eyes open; begin moving around den entrance
- Pack highly den-centered; all adults participate in feeding/guarding pups
- Pup weight: ~8–12 lbs (3.6–5.4 kg) by end of month
- Elk calving begins (late May) — vulnerable calves become available prey
- Pack territory defense continues but less aggressive
- Movement: 5–15 km/day; hunting forays return to den

### June
- Pups begin eating meat brought by adults (regurgitated or carried)
- Pups venture further from den but still den-centered
- Pack moves to first rendezvous site if den area resources decline
- Elk calving peak — pack may shift to targeting calves
- Movement: 8–18 km/day; gradually expanding

### July
- **Rendezvous site phase.** Pack moves pups to open meadow rendezvous sites
- Pups left at rendezvous with babysitter(s) while pack hunts
- Pup weight: ~25–35 lbs (11–16 kg)
- Pups begin play-fighting, establishing dominance hierarchy
- Multiple rendezvous sites may be used through summer
- Movement of hunting adults: 15–25 km/day; returning to rendezvous

### August
- Pups increasingly mobile; beginning to follow adults on short forays
- Rendezvous sites still in use
- Pack may begin to coalesce more as pups gain stamina
- Elk antler velvet shedding; bulls becoming aggressive (pre-rut)
- Movement: 15–25 km/day

### September
- **Fall transition.** Pups (~5 months old, ~45–55 lbs / 20–25 kg) begin traveling with pack
- Pack abandons rendezvous sites; begins wider territorial movements
- Elk rut underway — bulls distracted, cows grouped, calves vulnerable
- Increased kill rate as pack exploits rut-weakened bulls
- Movement: 20–35 km/day; territory-wide patrols resume

### October
- Full pack mobility; pups keeping up on most hunts
- Territory boundary marking intensifies (fall/winter territorial period)
- Pack encounters with neighboring packs increase
- Dispersal-age wolves (yearlings) may begin exploring territory edges
- Movement: 20–35 km/day

### November
- **Winter hunting.** Pack fully operational; pups (~7 months) participating in hunts
- Deep snow beginning; wolves have advantage over ungulates
- Bison become more accessible as they are slowed by snow
- First yearling dispersals may begin
- Movement: 25–40 km/day; following prey to winter range

### December
- Peak winter hunting efficiency; pack cohesion at annual high
- Elk concentrated on winter range; easier to locate
- Pack scent-marking and howling increase in preparation for breeding season
- Yearlings who will disperse become increasingly peripheral to pack
- Movement: 25–40 km/day

## 6. Health Indicators Tracked in the Field

### 6.1 Visual Body Condition

Assessed from field observation, camera traps, or during capture:
- Rib visibility, spine prominence, hip bone protrusion
- Rump musculature fullness
- Belly tuck (thinness) vs. belly distension (good condition or parasites)
- Fat deposits along brisket, rump, tail base

### 6.2 Coat/Pelage Quality

- **Wolves:** Dense winter coat (November–March); summer shedding (patchy May–July); dull/thin coat indicates malnutrition or disease
- **Bears:** Thick coat pre-hibernation; thin/patchy post-emergence; rubbing trees help remove winter coat
- **Elk:** Sleek summer coat (reddish-brown); thick winter coat (dark brown/gray); poor coat = stress indicator
- **Cougars:** Short, tawny year-round; scarring from fights visible on face/shoulders

### 6.3 Sarcoptic Mange (Wolves and Canids)

**Causative agent:** Sarcoptes scabiei mite (burrows into skin)

**Progression:**
1. **Early (weeks 1–4):** Mild hair loss starting on tail, rump, and hind legs. Increased scratching. Skin reddened.
2. **Moderate (weeks 4–12):** Hair loss spreads to flanks, back, and shoulders. Skin thickened and crusty (hyperkeratosis). Intense scratching causes secondary wounds.
3. **Severe (weeks 12+):** Widespread alopecia (>50% body). Thick crusty lesions over most of body. Emaciation from energy loss (thermoregulation). Skin infections from secondary bacteria.
4. **Terminal:** Complete hair loss. Hypothermia (cannot thermoregulate). Starvation. Death from exposure/sepsis within weeks if untreated.

**Field observation cues:** Patchy hair loss (especially tail and hind end first); scratching/biting at skin; crusty/scabby skin visible at distance; emaciation; lethargy.

**Recovery:** Some wolves do recover spontaneously (immune response kills mites). Recovery takes 3–6 months. Hair regrows but may be different texture/color initially.

### 6.4 Injuries

| Type | Species Most Affected | Field Signs |
|------|----------------------|-------------|
| Trap/snare injuries | Wolves, cougars, bears | Limping, missing toes/foot, scarring around leg |
| Vehicle strikes | Elk, deer, wolves, cougars | Limping, asymmetric gait, visible wounds, death |
| Intraspecific aggression | Wolves (pack fights), bears (mating competition), cougars (territorial) | Facial scarring, torn ears, limping, puncture wounds |
| Porcupine quills | Wolves, cougars | Quills embedded in muzzle/face; infection if not shed |
| Prey injuries | Wolves (kicked by elk/bison), cougars (gored by antlers) | Broken ribs, jaw fractures, limping |

### 6.5 Diseases

**Chronic Wasting Disease (CWD) — Elk and Deer:**
- Prion disease; always fatal; no treatment
- Incubation: 16–36 months (animal appears healthy but is infectious)
- Clinical signs: progressive weight loss, behavioral changes (listlessness, lowered head, drooping ears), excessive salivation, difficulty swallowing, increased drinking/urination, ataxia (stumbling), head tremors
- Death: within several months of symptom onset (rarely >1 year)
- Transmission: direct contact, contaminated environment (prions persist in soil for years)
- Note: Wolves cannot be infected by CWD prions; they can consume infected animals without contracting the disease

**Canine Distemper Virus (CDV) — Wolves:**
- Viral disease; often fatal in wolves
- Symptoms: nasal/ocular discharge, coughing, lethargy, seizures, behavioral changes (loss of fear of humans)
- Pups and yearlings most vulnerable (60–80% mortality in naive packs)
- Outbreak can devastate a pack in weeks
- No treatment in wild; survivors develop immunity

**Brucellosis — Elk and Bison:**
- Bacterial disease (Brucella abortus)
- Causes abortion in pregnant females, reduced fertility, joint infections
- Transmitted through birthing fluids and aborted fetuses
- Endemic in Greater Yellowstone Ecosystem elk and bison
- Field signs: late-term abortion, swollen joints (hygroma on knees), reduced calf recruitment

**Canine Parvovirus — Wolves:**
- Highly contagious viral disease
- Primarily affects pups < 6 months
- Symptoms: severe vomiting, bloody diarrhea, lethargy, rapid dehydration
- High mortality in pups (50–80%); adults usually survive
- Can reduce pack pup survival to near zero in outbreak years

## 7. Pack / Herd Dynamics

### 7.1 Wolf Pack Dynamics

**Breeding pair (not "alpha pair"):**
- Modern wolf biology has moved away from "alpha" terminology (based on captive studies)
- Wild packs are typically family units: breeding pair + their offspring from multiple years
- Breeding pair leads travel, initiates hunts, makes territorial decisions
- Breeding female chooses den site; breeding male provisions her during denning

**Pack composition changes:**
- **Births:** 4–6 pups/year; not all survive to fall (50–80% survival to 6 months in good years)
- **Pup mortality causes:** starvation, disease (parvo, distemper), predation (bears, eagles on very young pups), flooding of dens
- **Dispersal:** Yearlings and 2-year-olds leave pack; ~50% of wolves disperse by age 2
- **Immigration:** Lone dispersers occasionally join established packs (especially if a breeding position is vacant)
- **Death:** hunting/trapping (where legal), intraspecific killing (rival packs), disease, starvation, vehicle strikes

**Territory conflicts:**
- Pack encounters at territory boundaries are the leading natural cause of wolf mortality
- Larger packs typically dominate smaller packs
- Encounters range from howling standoffs to lethal attacks
- Losing pack may lose territory, den site, or pack members
- Buffer zones (1–5 km) between territories are heavily scent-marked but rarely occupied

### 7.2 Elk Herd Dynamics

- Winter herds: loose aggregations; no permanent leadership
- Cow-calf groups: led by experienced older cows who know migration routes and seasonal ranges
- Bull groups: bachelor herds in summer; solitary/harem holders during rut
- Rut hierarchy: largest-antlered, most experienced bulls hold harems; younger bulls ("satellite bulls") hover at periphery
- Calf recruitment: varies 10–40 calves per 100 cows depending on predation pressure and winter severity

### 7.3 Predator-Prey Interactions (Wolf-Elk System)

- Wolves test many elk before committing to a chase; success rate ~15–20% of chases
- Wolves target: calves, old/weak adults, injured individuals, individuals separated from herd
- Kill rate: approximately 1 elk per wolf per month (varies by pack size, prey density, season)
- Carcasses provide food for scavengers: ravens, magpies, coyotes, eagles, bears
- "Landscape of fear": elk avoid risky areas (riparian zones, forest edges) when wolves present, which has cascading ecological effects

## 10. Tag / Collar ID Conventions

### 10.1 Standard Format

Wildlife agencies and research projects use systematic ID conventions:

**General pattern:** `[SPECIES CODE]-[SEX]-[NUMBER]`

| Species | Code | Example ID | Meaning |
|---------|------|-----------|---------|
| Gray wolf | W | W-F-2847 | Wolf, female, individual #2847 |
| Grizzly bear | GB | GB-M-0512 | Grizzly bear, male, individual #0512 |
| Elk | E | E-F-1192 | Elk, female, individual #1192 |
| Mountain lion | ML | ML-M-0089 | Mountain lion, male, individual #0089 |
| Bald eagle | BE | BE-F-0341 | Bald eagle, female, individual #0341 |
| Loggerhead sea turtle | CC | CC-F-1087 | Caretta caretta, female, individual #1087 |
| Mule deer | MD | MD-M-0764 | Mule deer, male, individual #0764 |
| Bison | BI | BI-F-0203 | Bison, female, individual #0203 |

### 10.2 Yellowstone Wolf Project Convention

- Individual ID: sequential number + sex suffix (e.g., **1155M**, **907F**, **21M**, **42F**)
- Pack name: geographic feature (e.g., Lamar Canyon Pack, Junction Butte Pack, Mollie's Pack, Wapiti Lake Pack, 8-Mile Pack)
- Collared wolves referenced by number in all reports and communications
- Famous wolves known by number: **21M** (legendary Druid Peak alpha male), **06F** ("The 06 Female," Lamar Canyon alpha), **832F**, **755M**

### 10.3 Ear Tags and Auxiliary Markers

- **Colored ear tags:** Left/right ear, specific colors denote capture year or study
  - Example: "Orange right ear tag" = captured in 2022 cohort
  - Example: "Blue left, yellow right" = specific population study
- **PIT tags:** Passive Integrated Transponder; implanted subcutaneously. 10–15 digit alphanumeric code (e.g., 982.000012345678). Read with handheld scanner at <10 cm range.
- **Flipper tags (sea turtles):** Metal or plastic tags on front flippers. Monel or Inconel metal tags with stamped numbers (e.g., "QQM-847"). Include return address for tag recovery reporting.
- **Leg bands (eagles):** USGS Bird Banding Laboratory aluminum band on tarsus. 9-digit number (e.g., 0629-73841). Color bands for field ID without recapture (e.g., green over red on left leg = specific individual).
- **Freeze brands / hot brands (elk):** Large numbers branded on hip for identification at distance through spotting scope. Used less commonly now due to welfare concerns.

### 10.4 Naming Conventions for Reports

In formal reports:
- First mention: "W-F-2847 (adult female gray wolf, Cedar Creek Pack, GPS/Iridium collar deployed 2023-03-14)"
- Subsequent mentions: "W-F-2847" or "2847"
- Pack references: "the Cedar Creek Pack (n=8)"
- Uncollared animals: "uncollared gray adult" or "uncollared black yearling"
- Unknown sex: "W-U-####" (U = unknown)
- Cubs/pups (uncollared): "COY" (cub of the year) for bears; "pup" for wolves; not individually numbered until captured
